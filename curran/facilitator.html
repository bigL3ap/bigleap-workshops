Facilitator guide

For Zach, Dallas and Patrick. The instruction slides were pulled out of the deck on purpose, so the room hears this from you rather than reading it off a wall.

The one rule

Don't give them your examples first. If you open with a problem you already know about, the group will repeat it back to you. Let them name something real that is happening at Curran this week, then help them sharpen it.

Groups

Mixed on purpose. Each group should contain people who sit next to each other in the customer journey, so the handoff between them is in the room. Aim for someone from marketing, sales, operations and service in every group.

Set it up

3 min
Roughly thisCongratulations — you're all hired onto the RevOps team. Here's what we actually do on day one with a client: we dig into the processes, the systems, the departments, and how they hand work to each other. That's what you're about to do for your own company.

Then give them the worked example, so they know what "specific enough" looks like:

Zach's exampleWe had a problem passing information from the person who qualified a lead to the person who closed it. The closer sat through an hour-long handoff meeting and spent two to three hours building a proposal. We put an AI notetaker across the organization and had it populate notes against criteria we set. Now a closer preps for a closing call in fifteen minutes instead of three hours — and the client stopped having to repeat themselves. One workflow. That's the size of thing we're looking for.

Find the friction

15 min
  • Everyone scans the code and adds what they see, one at a time. Phones only — no one has to speak yet.
  • Walk the group while they type. The useful question is "when did that last happen?" — it turns a complaint into a specific moment.
  • If a group stalls, prompt across the handoff: "You're in marketing and you're in sales — what goes missing between you two?"
  • Seven minutes in, check in. If a group is stuck, swap them to describing a single customer from first contact to delivery.
  • Their answers appear on their own phone, and on the wall for the room.

Pick one, then build the fix

10 min
  • One problem per group, not three. Make them argue it out and agree on the single one worth solving.
  • Move straight into the Fix-It Builder — it is the same activity continued, not a new one. Their friction answers are already waiting in the picker.
  • Push them to name the trigger, what the system does, who owns it, and how you'd know it worked. Vague is fine to start; specific is the goal.
  • They're building toward a short pitch: here's the problem, here's our fix, here's how it would work.

Share it

10 min
  • Each group presents their one idea. Keep it to a couple of minutes each.
  • Then the room votes on the ideas across all three groups.
  • There's a prize — Big Leap duffel bags — for the group whose idea wins.
  • Say the real thing out loud: if an idea is good, it can go to their leadership and onto the roadmap. That's what turns them from an audience into collaborators.

If it goes sideways

  • Nobody types. Take one answer verbally and enter it yourself on the wall. It breaks the seal.
  • Someone blames a person or a team. Redirect to the system: "so what would have had to be true for that not to land on them?"
  • Wi-Fi dies. Paper and pens. Capture it on the wall afterwards — the activity is the conversation, not the tool.
  • A group finishes early. Ask for a second problem, or have them pressure-test another group's fix.
