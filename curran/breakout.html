Step 1 · Everyone, on your own phone

Find the friction

Think of a real moment with a customer or a project where something got stuck. Add as many as you like, one at a time. Describe the situation, not a person.

How to do this step

Everyone does this one, on their own phone. Think of one real thing that went wrong or took too long in the last month. Write it in your own words, then tap what kind of problem it is and what it costs. Then add another.

  • “A designer asks for three swatches. The request sits in the shared inbox over the weekend because nobody owns it.”
  • “Sales promises a rush install date, but operations only finds out when the order lands, so we have to call the customer back.”

Facilitators: don't give your own example first — they'll just hand it back to you. If a group is quiet, ask: “walk one customer from first contact to install, and tell me where it gets slow.”

    Step 2 · One phone per group

    Pick your group's top problem

    How to do this step

    One phone now drives, and the group talks it through. Read your group's answers together and pick the one problem that is either the most expensive or happens the most often. You can't fix everything — pick one.

    • Good pick: “swatch requests sit unowned” — happens weekly, costs sales.
    • Weak pick: “the printer jams” — annoying, but it isn't a customer problem.

    Facilitators: if the group is split, ask “which one would the customer notice first?” and go with that.

    These are everyone's answers from step 1. As a group, tap the one problem you want to fix. Steps 2 to 5 are shared, so everyone in your group sees the same choice on their phone.

    Step 3 · Design the fix

    Design the fix

    How to do this step

    Describe your fix as a recipe in four parts: when it starts, what happens automatically, who is in charge, and how you'd know it worked. Just tap the options that fit — you're not writing software.

    • When a swatch request comes in → it's assigned to a named rep and they get a reminder → Sales is in charge → we'd know it worked because no request sits unanswered.

    Facilitators: keep them out of the weeds. If someone starts naming HubSpot fields, pull back to “what should just happen, on its own?”

    Step 4 · Human or robot

    Who does each part?

    How to do this step

    Go through each part of your fix and decide who should do it: a person, a system, or both. The rule of thumb: if it's typing, copying, chasing or reminding, a system should do it. If it needs judgement or a relationship, a person should.

    • System: logging the request, assigning it, sending the “we got it” reply, reminding the rep on day three.
    • Person: calling the designer to talk through the project and what will actually work in their space.

    Facilitators: if they mark everything “person”, ask which part they'd happily never do again.

    For each part of your fix, tap who should do it.
    Robot: the system does it on its own. Human: a person needs to do it. Both: the system does the legwork, a person makes the call.

    Your proposal is written for you below — you just give it a short, memorable name and tap share. Then one person presents it to the room in three parts: here's the problem, here's our fix, here's how it would work.
